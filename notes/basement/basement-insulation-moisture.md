# Basement: Retrofit Insulation and Moisture Protection (etterisolering og fuktsikring av kjellere)

Summary of the video [PROFFPRATEN: Kjeller og fukt (med Trond Bøhlerengen fra SINTEF)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TA63vPSUsIU), Isola Norge (Norwegian, about 21 min). Speakers: Karl, product manager for Platon products at Isola, and Trond Bøhlerengen, senior adviser at SINTEF.

## Key message

Buying an old house with a large basement does **not** mean the basement can simply be turned into living space. **First check the moisture situation.** Exterior moisture protection (drainage) comes first; interior insulation only works if the walls and floor are already dry.

## Summary by chapter

### 1. What is a basement? (Hva defineres som en kjeller?)

- A basement (kjeller) is a room below ground level, normally not meant for living: storage rooms (boder), garage, laundry room, old potato cellars.
- On sloping sites part of the basement wall is above ground, allowing windows, daylight and escape routes. Those rooms can become living space (an underetasje), while the rooms at the back against the hill stay as storage.

### 2. Handling surface water (Håndtering av overflatevann)

- Water comes from two sources: **moisture in the ground** (byggegrunn), which you can't see, and **surface water**: rain and runoff from roofs, terraces and access balconies (svalganger).
- The building code has long required that the ground **slopes away from the house** (fall vekk fra huset).
- Lead as little surface water as possible down along the basement walls. Don't put free-draining gravel right up to the surface next to the foundation wall; use a **tight layer at the top sloping away from the house** instead.

### 3. Building materials in basements (Byggematerialer brukt i kjeller)

- Old houses: **natural stone** (naturstein) foundation walls.
- Concrete (betong) came into use after about the First World War.
- From the 1950s: **lightweight clay aggregate blocks** (lettklinkerblokker, brand name **Leca**), 15–25 cm, where the block itself is both load-bearing and insulating.
- From the 1980s–90s: blocks with built-in insulation (**isoblokk**).
- The 1949 and 1969 building codes already required floors to stop moisture as water and vapour. As basements in 1960s–80s Husbanken-financed houses were fitted out as living space, a **moisture barrier (fuktsperre)** indirectly became a requirement.

### 4. Living space in a basement: important checks (Boligrom i kjeller – viktige avklaringer)

- Many old basements (especially pre-1960) are completely unsuitable for living space.
- **Check for moisture first**: salt or lime deposits (saltutslag, kalkutslag) on walls and floor mean water is coming through, and the exterior moisture protection is failing.
- **Drainage does not last forever.** Many old houses need new drainage. When digging up anyway, it pays to insulate the outside too.
- **Don't dig deeper than the old foundation** when re-draining, or the building may settle (setning). So foundations and interior load-bearing walls may still stand in damp ground.

### 5. Insulation and exterior moisture protection (Isolering og utvendig fuktsikring)

- On the **outside you can add as much insulation as you like**.
- Against the ground, insulation also works as **moisture protection**, because it is **capillary-breaking** (kapillærbrytende), both on walls and under floors.
- Best: insulate the outside, ideally the **full wall height**. If that isn't possible above ground, still insulate the outside below ground.
- If the wall above ground isn't insulated outside: a **concrete wall is a big thermal bridge** (kuldebro) and also needs interior insulation for the full height. A **Leca or isoblokk wall** insulates fairly well itself and may not need it.

### 6. Placing the dimpled membrane (Plassering av knasteplate)

- **Knasteplate / grunnmursplate** = dimpled foundation membrane (e.g. Platon, the green one; Platon invented it with a patent from the mid-1960s).
- Traditional recommendation: place it **directly on the wall**. Drawback: it is **vapour-tight** (diffusjonstett), so the wall cannot dry outwards.
- If the wall needs to dry outwards: use **vapour-open insulation** directly on the wall and put the dimpled membrane **outside the insulation** as mechanical protection.
- The membrane is closed at the top with a finishing strip, so it is not ventilated. Ventilating behind exterior insulation would reduce the insulation's effect, so it isn't recommended.
- End it **below ground**: it is plastic and **UV light** weakens it.
- Always make a good top finish detail. If it's outside the insulation, use flashings or a membrane to cover the gap back to the wall so nothing runs down between the layers.

### 7. Stopping moisture from the inside? (Stoppe fukten fra innsiden?)

- If you can't dig up outside, first check for signs of moisture coming through. If the drainage is failing, fitting out the room without fixing the outside is irresponsible.
- **Emergency solution (nødløsning)**: mount a dimpled membrane on the inside of the wet wall as a moisture barrier, let the outer wall "live its own life", and build an insulated wall inside it. Not in the Byggforsk series, but acceptable when the alternative is not using the room at all.
- Needs **good sealing all the way**, including wall–ceiling transitions (e.g. the blue Platon system).

### 8. Using "black felt" (Bruk av "svartpapp")

- In the 1960s–70s basements were often fitted out with **black felt (svartpapp)** on the foundation wall, a timber stud wall (bindingsverk) with insulation, **plastic sheeting** (plastfolie) as vapour barrier, and cladding.
- This traps moisture from both sides: a "whitewashed grave" (kalket grav). Moisture entering the timber can't get out, which leads to **mould, fungus and rot** (mugg, sopp og råte). **Don't build like this.** If you find an old wall built this way with no damage, you were lucky.

### 9. Moisture protection of basement floors (Fuktsikring av kjellergulv)

- Room height is often the limit, but **any insulation is far better than none**: even 5 cm under a new floor helps a lot.
- Always assume high relative humidity in the ground; moisture moves up by **diffusion**. So you also need a **diffusion barrier** (diffusjonssperre), in practice a **plastic sheet**, as the floor's moisture barrier.
- Standard solution: **insulation as capillary break + plastic sheeting as moisture barrier**.
- Alternative: **Platon floor products / Platon Multi**: a pressure-resistant polypropylene dimpled sheet that is both moisture barrier and floor underlay; the new floor can go straight on top.

### 10. Wet rooms in the basement (Våtrom i kjeller)

- Walls below ground are the most moisture-exposed parts of a house, and wet rooms (bathrooms) are the most moisture-exposed rooms.
- **No timber or other organic materials** in wet room walls against the ground.
- Use e.g. plastic-based insulation with render (puss), or half Leca façade blocks as insulation and a solid base, then a waterproof membrane and tiles.

## Summary checklist (Oppsummering)

1. **Get rid of surface water**: ground sloping away from the house; lead water from roofs, balconies, terraces and access balconies away over the ground, not down along the wall.
2. **Working drainage**: when re-draining, backfill with free-draining material and insulate the outside. Exterior drainage and moisture protection are the "alpha and omega".
3. **Interior-only insulation** is possible only if walls and floor are already dry; then follow the Byggforsk series solutions.
